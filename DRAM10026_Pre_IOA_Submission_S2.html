This submission is the written starting point for your five minutes in the Interactive Oral Assessment — a set of considered notes towards a theatre review of one particular dimension of two productions, which you will elaborate and defend in the room.

What this is

Draft notes towards a review

What you submit here is a 600-word draft of notes towards a theatre review of your allotted focus area, examined comparatively across both MTC productions. A theatre review, at its fullest, combines three things: correct information, critical reflection, and a sense of the value, quality, or successful execution of what you saw. Your notes are working towards that combination.

This is a first draft, not a finished essay. We are reading it as a good-faith set of developing arguments, topics, and themes — evidence that you have watched closely, formed some judgements, and begun to marshal the specifics to support them. The five minutes you spend speaking in the IOA will build directly on these notes — extending them, testing them against your peers, opening them to questions — so the more thought you put in here, the more you will have to work with in the room. It does not need to be polished; it needs to be genuinely yours and genuinely thought through.

What to include

What your 600 words should contain

Your notes should work through the following. Treat this as a checklist rather than a running order — the shape is yours to decide.

  • A two-to-three sentence summary of each play.
  • Some context on the origin of each play, and of this particular production.
  • An accurate list of the key creatives and their roles.
  • Your assessment of the strengths (up to three examples) and weaknesses (up to three examples) of the productions.
  • With your assigned focus area in mind, an evaluation of how well it was executed — and how this was similar or different across the two productions.
Remember

These are notes indicating your developing ideas. They may be in dot or bullet points, and will be read as good-faith first-draft arguments, topics and themes — the raw material you will elaborate on in the IOA itself.

Before you write

Questions to think with

Before you draft your notes, it helps to think loosely and widely about what you saw. None of these questions need to appear in your submission — they are here to loosen your responses and surface the observations worth writing about.

First responses
  • Prepare a brief synopsis (around 250 words) of each play for your own reference.
  • Recall and record your immediate, gut responses to each production, before you start refining them.
  • What, specifically, did you love about either — story, characterisation, performance, set, costume, lighting, dialogue, theme, tone, imagery, originality of interpretation? How did each element work on its own, and how did they work in concert?
Going deeper
  • What fascinated you — something you wanted to know more about, that the production perhaps skipped over?
  • What gave you pause — a moment that took you out of the world of the play, a problem of credibility or suspended disbelief?
  • What did you expect of the productions? What surprised you? What was remarkable?
  • Did they meet your expectations — or those of the audience, or of the creative team? How might you measure or know this?
  • Note down questions you might ask the creative teams if you had the chance, and questions you want to raise with your peers.
Towards judgement
  • What was original, vital, urgent, relevant, daring, formally audacious — or inconsistent, or ill-conceived? And, importantly, why? Accrue the evidence.
  • Beyond a thumbs-up or a star rating, how would you evaluate each show, and why?

These adjectives sit along a spectrum and are useful here. What others have utility in a review — without gathering cruel, scornful, or pejorative connotations?

What the review should do

Make an argument, anchored in specifics

Your task is to make an argument about how your focus area operated across the two productions. Where did each show succeed in this dimension and where did it fall short? Where were the two productions doing comparable work and where did they part ways? You are not describing what you saw; you are evaluating it — taking a position and defending it with specific evidence drawn from particular moments, choices, and effects.

Be concrete. The strongest theatre criticism moves between the specific and the general: a single observation about a lighting cue, a costume choice, a vocal delivery, an entrance, becomes the anchor for a larger claim about what the production is trying to do and whether it succeeds. General praise or general complaint does very little work. The closer your writing stays to actual moments in the productions, the more convincing your argument becomes.

You will need some orientation for the reader — what each play is, who made this production, when and where it sits — but weave this in as the writing requires it rather than front-loading a synopsis. Assume your reader is informed but hasn't necessarily seen these particular productions. Names of key creatives relevant to your focus area should appear naturally where you discuss their work.

A working principle

If a sentence could be lifted out and applied to any production of anything, it is not yet doing the work of criticism. Push toward the observation only these two productions could prompt.

Register and form

Write as a critic, whatever the form

There is no required format for this submission. You may write it as continuous prose, as an essay, or in structured dot points — whichever helps you think clearly and set out your argument. We have no preference: it is your working document, and what matters is the quality of the thinking, not its shape on the page.

Whichever form you choose, aim for the tone of serious arts criticism rather than academic essay-writing: direct, evaluative, willing to make judgements, attentive to language. Move beyond general observation to tell us why certain choices worked and others less well. Avoid baseless praise as much as gratuitous harshness — a judgement is only worth as much as the evidence behind it.

On length

600 words is the ceiling, not the target

A tight 500-word review with a sharpened argument is stronger than 600 words that pad out to the limit. What we are looking for is precision of attention and clarity of judgement, not volume. Cut anything that is not earning its place.

Your submission is the considered version of your thinking. The IOA is where you bring that thinking into a room with others and let it move.
